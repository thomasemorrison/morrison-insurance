## Week 1 — September 21–27

### Primary: When the skies clear, hazards can remain: a simple fall readiness checklist

**Why it helps locally.** September is National Preparedness Month, and a plain-language checklist remains useful across Center, Shelby County, and Deep East Texas without suggesting a storm is forecast. It helps households prepare before severe weather and make safer first decisions after rain, wind, flooding, or a power interruption. [1] [2]

**Factual basis and source URL.** Ready.gov advises households to make a plan, build a kit, safeguard important documents, sign up for local warnings, and avoid floodwater. It says to return after a flood only when authorities say it is safe. The National Weather Service adds that downed lines, damaged buildings, and unsafe generator use remain hazards after the skies clear. Texas emergency-management guidance directs residents to official local notifications, 911 for life-threatening emergencies, 2-1-1 Texas for community resources, and DriveTexas for road conditions. [1] [2] [3] [4]

**Content type and 4:5 creative direction.** Produce a five-to-seven-panel **4:5 carousel** or one saveable checklist graphic. Use a permission-cleared, non-dramatic local scene: a household tote holding a weather radio, flashlight, first-aid supplies, bottled water, and a waterproof document pouch. Do not show damage, active flooding, addresses, plates, fear-based imagery, or an active emergency.

**Platform-neutral content outline.** Start with the question, “What should a household set up before the next round of rough weather?” Explain the five actions: know alerts and contacts; make a household plan; gather several days of supplies; protect paper and digital documents; and keep people, cars, and generators out of floodwater or unsafe spaces. Follow with a concise after-storm panel: wait for officials, avoid downed lines and wet electrical equipment, and use portable generators only outdoors and away from openings. End with official local-resource links rather than insurance language. [1] [2] [3] [4]

**Low-pressure next step.** Invite readers to choose one small task: place key documents in a waterproof container, update a household contact, or save the county emergency-management and 2-1-1 information.

**Fact owner or reviewer.** **Fact owners:** Ready.gov, National Weather Service, and Texas Division of Emergency Management. **Required reviewers:** Unified Content Desk fact reviewer; Morrison approver; local-contact verifier for Shelby County Emergency Management details.

**Publication trigger.** The desk confirms that no local forecast, warning, or incident is being implied; rechecks Shelby County Emergency Management contact details; and clears the final creative and accessibility text. If active weather occurs, the user-owned weather workflow takes precedence and this item does not become a routine weather post.

## Week 3 — October 5–11

### Primary: A little more attention for the deer—and for one another

**Why it helps locally.** This is a practical road-safety reminder for drivers in Center, Shelby County, and Deep East Texas as fall travel and low-light conditions continue. It gives clear actions that are useful whether someone drives rural roads or town routes, without claiming a local crash rate, collision probability, or insurance outcome. [11]

**Factual basis and source URL.** TxDOT says deer can be encountered almost anywhere in Texas, often travel in groups, and may be more active during fall mornings and evenings. Its driver guidance is to reduce speed when deer are near the road, use high beams only when no oncoming traffic is present, brake firmly while staying in the lane, and after a deer strike move safely off the road, call 911, and remain in the vehicle rather than approach the animal. TPWD lists Shelby County archery-only white-tailed deer season beginning October 3, but the hunting date is context only and does not predict collisions. [11] [12]

**Content type and 4:5 creative direction.** Produce a five- or six-panel **4:5 safety carousel** or one vertically formatted checklist. Use a permission-cleared, quiet image of a wooded two-lane road at dawn or dusk, photographed from a safe stationary position, or a simple original illustration. Do not stage a deer in traffic, show a crash or injured wildlife, use incident imagery, or photograph while driving.

**Platform-neutral content outline.** Open with “What should you do when a deer appears near the road?” Explain the five actions in sequence: scan the road, shoulders, and tree line; expect more deer after seeing one; ease off speed; brake firmly and stay in lane; and use high beams at night only when safe and legal. Finish with the after-strike safety actions and a reminder to follow dispatcher and law-enforcement instructions. [11]

**Low-pressure next step.** Ask readers to save the checklist and share it with the person in their household who drives most often at dawn or after sunset.

**Fact owner or reviewer.** **Fact owners:** Texas Department of Transportation and Texas Parks & Wildlife Department. **Required reviewers:** Unified Content Desk fact reviewer; Morrison approver; final roadway-safety reviewer.

**Publication trigger.** The desk confirms that all driving actions match the live TxDOT guidance, the high-beam qualification remains intact, and the creative has no staged roadway danger. The item must not frame the October 3 hunting date as a crash prediction.

**Factual basis and source URL.** As of September 16, USDA APHIS listed current Texas New World screwworm zone actions, while the Texas Animal Health Commission described restrictions in designated parts of Texas and the reporting process for suspected cases. **Platform-neutral content outline.** Ask, “When should an animal owner make a call rather than guess?” Explain that suspected wounds or larvae should be assessed by a veterinarian and reported using current agency directions. State the wildlife distinction. Clearly say not to diagnose from a fly, collect larvae independently, self-treat, or transport a suspected animal without direction. Include the exact reporting contact only if live verification confirms it. [14] [15]

**Low-pressure next step.** Invite readers to bookmark the APHIS and TAHC pages and discuss a normal animal-health observation routine with their veterinarian.

## Reusable pattern for educational posts

Use this five-part pattern for every review-ready educational item:

1. **Question.** Start with one plain local question, such as “What should you do when a deer appears near the road?”
2. **Practical explanation.** Give a short, verified answer that explains the action and its limit. Use a calm tone and one idea per panel.
3. **What to prepare or ask.** Offer one small preparation step or a question to take to an appropriate expert. Do not provide individualized insurance, veterinary, medical, legal, or emergency advice.
4. **Source and review date.** Name the primary official source, link it in the final asset or accompanying post, and record the desk’s factual review date. For changing sources, record the fresh-check date immediately before publication.
5. **Calm local next step.** Offer a low-pressure action such as saving a checklist, checking the organizer calendar, bookmarking an agency page, or thanking a local mentor.
