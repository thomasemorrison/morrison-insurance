# Research: Asked for a Certificate of Insurance? A Shelby County Business Checklist

Publication and research date: November 2, 2026. Editorial responsibility: Morrison Insurance. The article addresses an actual paperwork request for a contractor, vendor, or venue owner in Shelby County. It intentionally avoids repeating the existing broad business-insurance coverage introduction.

## What a certificate establishes—and what it cannot do

TDI describes certificates as proof that a person or organization has insurance coverage. The agency's index provides approved certificate forms and links to its FAQ and Texas Insurance Code Chapter 1811. [1]

Section 1811.001 defines a certificate as an insurer- or agent-executed statement or summary of property or casualty insurance issued to a third party outside the insurance contract. It excludes an insurance binder or policy form. Section 1811.151 says an approved, properly executed and issued certificate confirms that the referenced policy was issued or coverage was bound, even if it says it is for informational purposes only. This supports explaining the document as proof without presenting it as a coverage guarantee. [3]

Section 1811.152 states:

> “A certificate of insurance is not a policy of insurance and does not amend, extend, or alter the coverage afforded by the referenced insurance policy.” [3]

Sections 1811.051 and 1811.153 prohibit using a certificate to change policy coverage, convey contractual rights, or confer new or additional rights beyond the referenced policy or an executed endorsement. The article therefore says a coverage change must occur through the policy, such as an insurer-issued endorsement, rather than through words added to a certificate. It does not suggest that every request requires a new endorsement or that existing blanket coverage cannot apply. [3]

## Certificate holder versus additional insured

Section 1811.001 defines a certificate holder as a person other than the policyholder who is designated on the certificate or receives the certificate at the policyholder's request. Section 1811.153 explains why listing a holder does not create new insurance rights. TDI FAQ question 19 likewise says the holder box cannot imply or confer rights beyond the policy or executed endorsement. [2] [3]

TDI FAQ question 1 says a certificate cannot name the holder as an additional insured solely because the policy has a blanket additional-insured endorsement, although it may state that a blanket endorsement exists. Question 15 says the additional-insured box should be checked if an endorsement names the holder as an additional insured. The article deliberately preserves this Texas-specific caveat instead of claiming that a certificate checkmark grants coverage or that any blanket endorsement automatically applies. [2]

Question 3 confirms that Chapter 1811 does not prohibit an agent or insurer from providing the policy or endorsement copies. Asking about relevant supporting documents is therefore grounded in the official FAQ, not a claim that every recipient must receive an entire policy. [2]

## Contract requests and prohibited representations

Section 1811.154 prohibits a certificate from referring to legal or insurance requirements in a contract other than the underlying insurance contract, including construction or service contracts. TDI FAQ questions 4 and 10 explain the same rule. A job or contract number may identify the work, but language claiming coverage according to the other contract is not allowed. The article distinguishes collecting contract requirements for review from reproducing contractual compliance promises on the certificate. [2] [3]

Section 1811.054 prohibits requiring false or misleading certificate information. Section 1811.055 prohibits requiring other documents inconsistent with the chapter. TDI FAQ questions 17 and 18 address supplemental questionnaires and affidavits. The practical instruction is to send unfamiliar forms or requested wording to the agent, not to promise that a blanket affidavit or contract compliance statement can be issued. [2] [3]

The instruction not to sign a promise of compliance before reviewing requirements and policy documents is general risk-aware editorial guidance, not a determination of contract enforceability. The article refers readers to qualified legal guidance for obligations they are being asked to accept. It does not interpret indemnification clauses or give advice about accepting legal terms.

## Expiration and notice rights

Section 1811.155 requires both that the person be named in the policy or an endorsement and that the policy, endorsement, or Texas law or rule require notice. A certificate cannot alter notice terms. TDI FAQ question 5 describes the same conditions. The article does not promise cancellation notices simply because someone receives a certificate, and it does not invent a universal 30-day notice right. [2] [3]

Checking policy expiration, requesting updated evidence for work crossing renewal, retaining private records, and using verified agent/recipient channels are practical editorial recommendations. They are not represented as statutory deadlines, guaranteed processing times, or universal privacy-law requirements. No contracts, certificates, or policy numbers should be requested in public comments.
