## The Core Message

A cheaper premium is not always a better deal. When a policy is priced lower because it pays Actual Cash Value instead of Replacement Cost Value, the policyholder is taking on a hidden financial risk they may not understand until they file a claim. Our job is to make sure the people who follow us understand the difference — so they can make an informed choice, not just a cheap one.

### Hook
Two policies. Same house. Same coverage limits. One pays $18,000 after a hail storm. The other pays $11,000. The difference comes down to four letters buried in the policy language: ACV or RCV.

### Core Concept — Explained Simply

**Actual Cash Value (ACV)**
ACV pays what your property was worth at the time of the loss — after depreciation. The older your roof, your car, or your belongings, the less the insurance company pays. A 10-year-old roof that costs $20,000 to replace might only be worth $9,000 in depreciated value. That's what ACV pays. You cover the rest.

**Replacement Cost Value (RCV)**
RCV pays what it actually costs to replace the damaged property with something of like kind and quality — at today's prices. That same 10-year-old roof costs $20,000 to replace today. RCV pays $20,000 (minus your deductible). You're made whole.

**The simple version:**
> ACV = what it was worth. RCV = what it costs to fix.

### Sections to Cover

**1. The Roof Example**
This is the most common and most dramatic illustration for East Texas homeowners. Hail storms are real here. Walk through the math:
- 10-year-old roof, replacement cost $20,000
- Depreciation rate: roughly 5% per year = $10,000 in depreciation
- ACV payout: $10,000
- RCV payout: $20,000 (minus deductible)
- Out-of-pocket difference: $10,000

Most people don't have $10,000 sitting around after a storm. That's the real cost of the cheaper policy.

**2. The Auto Example**
ACV is standard on auto policies for total losses — that part is normal and expected. But the key is understanding it. If your vehicle is totaled, the insurance company pays what the car was worth the day before the accident — not what you paid for it, not what it costs to replace it with something comparable today.

Walk through a relatable example:
- 2018 pickup truck, purchased for $35,000
- Current market value (ACV): $22,000
- You still owe $26,000 on the loan
- Gap: $4,000 out of pocket — unless you have GAP coverage

This is where GAP insurance becomes part of the conversation. Mention it as a natural extension.

**3. Personal Property Inside the Home**
Many homeowners don't realize their personal property coverage can also be ACV or RCV. The difference matters:
- 5-year-old 65" TV, replacement cost $800
- ACV after depreciation: maybe $250
- RCV: $800

Multiply that across a house full of furniture, appliances, and electronics after a fire. The gap adds up fast.

**4. Why Some Policies Are Cheaper**
This is the honest, non-bashing explanation of why a competitor's quote might be lower:
- ACV policies cost less to insure because the insurance company pays out less at claim time
- Higher deductibles reduce the premium but increase out-of-pocket costs after a loss
- Lower coverage limits reduce the premium but leave gaps

We're not saying cheaper is always wrong. We're saying: know what you're buying. A policy that costs $200 less per year but pays $10,000 less after a claim is not a good deal.

**5. What to Ask When Comparing Policies**
Give readers a practical checklist:
1. Does my dwelling coverage pay ACV or RCV?
2. Does my personal property coverage pay ACV or RCV?
3. What is my deductible — and is there a separate wind/hail deductible?
4. If I have a car loan or lease, do I have GAP coverage?
5. When was the last time my coverage limits were reviewed against current rebuild costs?
